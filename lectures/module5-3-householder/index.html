1. Recap Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization¶

For many applications, we find ourselves interested in the column spaces of a matrix AAA:

⟨a1⟩⊆⟨a1,a2⟩⊆⟨a1,a2,a3⟩…\langle a_1 \rangle \subseteq \langle a_1, a_2 \rangle \subseteq \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle \ldots⟨a1​⟩⊆⟨a1​,a2​⟩⊆⟨a1​,a2​,a3​⟩…
(1)

The idea of QR factorization is the construction of a sequence of orthonormal vectors, q1,q2,…q_1, q_2, \ldotsq1​,q2​,… that span these successive spaces.

Thus, suppose we want to find an orthogonal basis for the span of the columns of AAA:

[a1∣a2]=[q1∣q2][r11r120r22]\Bigg[ a_1 \Bigg| a_2 \Bigg] = \Bigg[ q_1 \Bigg| q_2 \Bigg] \begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} \\ 0 & r_{22} \end{bmatrix}[a1​∣∣​a2​]=[q1​∣∣​q2​][r11​0​r12​r22​​]
(2)

Given a1,a2,…a_1, a_2, \dotsa1​,a2​,…, we can construct vectors q1,q2,…q_1, q_2, \ldotsq1​,q2​,… and entries rijr_{ij}rij​, by an iterative process of successive orthogonalization.

Gram-Schmidt with more parallelism¶

We are performing the following (from right to left):

w=(I−q2q2T)(I−q1q1T)v=(I−q1q1T−q2q2T+q2q2Tq1q1T)v=(I−[q1∣q2][q1Tq2T])v\begin{align} w = (I - q_2 q_2^T) (I - q_1 q_1^T) v &= (I - q_1 q_1^T - q_2 q_2^T + q_2 q_2^T q_1 q_1^T) v \\ &= \Bigg( I - \Big[ q_1 \Big| q_2 \Big] \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T \\ q_2^T \end{bmatrix} \Bigg) v \end{align}w=(I−q2​q2T​)(I−q1​q1T​)v​=(I−q1​q1T​−q2​q2T​+q2​q2T​q1​q1T​)v=(I−[q1​∣∣​q2​][q1T​q2T​​])v​​
(3)

which can be factored in blocks to expoloit a bit of parallelism. Let’s call this classical Gram-Schmidt.

function gram_schmidt_classical(A)
    m, n = size(A)
    Q = zeros(m, n)
    R = zeros(n, n)
    for j in 1:n
        v = A[:,j]
        R[1:j-1,j] = Q[:,1:j-1]' * v
        v -= Q[:,1:j-1] * R[1:j-1,j]
        R[j,j] = norm(v)
        Q[:,j] = v / R[j,j]
    end
    Q, R
end

Classical Vs Modified Gram-Schmidt¶

Why does the order of operations matter?¶

We are performing the following (from right to left):

w=(I−q2q2T)(I−q1q1T)v=(I−q1q1T−q2q2T+q2q2Tq1q1T)v=(I−[q1∣q2][q1Tq2T])v\begin{align} w = (I - q_2 q_2^T) (I - q_1 q_1^T) v &= (I - q_1 q_1^T - q_2 q_2^T + q_2 q_2^T q_1 q_1^T) v \\ &= \Bigg( I - \Big[ q_1 \Big| q_2 \Big] \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T \\ q_2^T \end{bmatrix} \Bigg) v \end{align}w=(I−q2​q2T​)(I−q1​q1T​)v​=(I−q1​q1T​−q2​q2T​+q2​q2T​q1​q1T​)v=(I−[q1​∣∣​q2​][q1T​q2T​​])v​​
(4)

which can be factored in blocks and is not exact in finite arithmetic.

Let’s look at this a bit more closely. In the classical Gram-Schmidt (CGS), we take each vector, one at a time, and make it orthogonal to all previous vectors. If an error is made in computing q2q_2q2​ in CGS, so that q1Tq2=δq^T_1q_2=\deltaq1T​q2​=δ is small, but non-zero in finite arithmetic, this will not be corrected for in any of the computations that follow, and it will actually be propagated. In this case, v3v_3v3​ will not be orthogonal to q1q_1q1​ or q2q_2q2​.

Because of rounding errors, in classical Gram-Schmidt, Qk−1Q_{k−1}Qk−1​ does not have truly orthogonal columns. In modified Gram–Schmidt (MGS) we compute the length of the projection of w=vkw = v_kw=vk​ onto q1q_1q1​ and subtract that projection (and the rounding errors) from www. Next, we compute the length of the projection of the computed www onto q2q_2q2​ and subtract that projection (and the rounding errors) from www, and so on, but always orthogonalizing against the computed version of www.

Right-looking modified Gram-Schmidt¶

Each outer step of the modified Gram-Schmidt algorithm can be interpreted as a right-multiplication by a square upper-triangular matrix.

function gram_schmidt_modified(A)
    m, n = size(A)
    Q = copy(A)
    R = zeros(n, n)
    for j in 1:n
        R[j,j] = norm(Q[:,j])
        Q[:,j] /= R[j,j]
        R[j,j+1:end] = Q[:,j]'*Q[:,j+1:end]
        Q[:,j+1:end] -= Q[:,j]*R[j,j+1:end]'
    end
    Q, R
end

2. Householder triangularization¶

Householder triangularization is numerically more stable than Gram-Schmidt orthogonaliztion, though it lacks the latter’s applicability as a basis for iterative methods. The Householder algorithm is a process of “orthogonal triangularization”, making a matrix triangular by a sequence of unitary matrix operations.

Gram-Schmidt constructed a triangular matrix RRR to orthogonalize AAA into QQQ. Each step was an orthogonal projector, which is a rank-deficient operation.

Image credits: Pictures taken from the reference book: Trefethen and Bau (1997), Numerical Linear Algebra (available at the library).

Householder uses orthogonal transformations (reflectors) to triangularize.

Qn⋯Q1⏟QTA=R\underbrace{Q_{n} \dotsb Q_1}_{Q^T} A = RQTQn​⋯Q1​​​A=R
(5)

The reflection, as depicted above by Trefethen and Bau (1997) can be written F=I−2vvTvTvF = I - 2 \frac{v v^T}{v^T v}F=I−2vTvvvT​.

The structure of the algorithm is

[∗∗∗∗∗∗∗∗∗∗∗∗∗∗∗]⏟A→[∗∗∗0∗∗0∗∗0∗∗0∗∗]⏟Q1A→[∗∗∗0∗∗00∗00∗00∗]⏟Q2Q1A→[∗∗∗0∗∗00∗000000]⏟Q3Q2Q1A\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} * & * & * \\ * & * & * \\ * & * & * \\ * & * & * \\ * & * & * \\ \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \to \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} * & * & * \\ 0 & * & * \\ 0 & * & * \\ 0 & * & * \\ 0 & * & * \\ \end{bmatrix}}_{Q_1 A} \to \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} * & * & * \\ 0 & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & * \\ 0 & 0 & * \\ 0 & 0 & * \\ \end{bmatrix}}_{Q_2 Q_1 A} \to \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} * & * & * \\ 0 & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & * \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}}_{Q_3 Q_2 Q_1 A}A⎣⎡​∗∗∗∗∗​∗∗∗∗∗​∗∗∗∗∗​⎦⎤​​​→Q1​A⎣⎡​∗0000​∗∗∗∗∗​∗∗∗∗∗​⎦⎤​​​→Q2​Q1​A⎣⎡​∗0000​∗∗000​∗∗∗∗∗​⎦⎤​​​→Q3​Q2​Q1​A⎣⎡​∗0000​∗∗000​∗∗∗00​⎦⎤​​​
(6)

Constructing the QjQ_jQj​¶

Qn−1⋯Q0⏟QTA=R\underbrace{Q_{n-1} \dotsb Q_0}_{Q^T} A = RQTQn−1​⋯Q0​​​A=R
(7)

Each of our QjQ_jQj​ will have the form

Qj=[Ij00F]Q_j = \begin{bmatrix} I_j & 0 \\ 0 & F \end{bmatrix}Qj​=[Ij​0​0F​]
(8)

where FFF is a “reflection” that achieves

Fx=[∥x∥00⋮]F x = \begin{bmatrix} \lVert x \rVert \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}Fx=⎣⎡​∥x∥00⋮​⎦⎤​
(9)

where xxx is the column of RRR from the diagonal down. This transformation is a reflection across a plane with normal v=Fx−x=∥x∥e1−xv = Fx - x = \lVert x \rVert e_1 - xv=Fx−x=∥x∥e1​−x.

The two methods can be summarized as follows:

  • Gram-Schmidt: a triangular orthogonalization

  • Householder: an orthogonal triangularization

Householder: A naive algorithm¶

function qr_householder_naive(A)
    m, n = size(A)
    R = copy(A)
    V = [] # list of reflectors
    for j in 1:n
        v = copy(R[j:end, j])
        v[1] -= norm(v)
        v = normalize(v)
        R[j:end,j:end] -= 2 * v * (v' * R[j:end,j:end])
        push!(V, v)
    end
    V, R
end

How to interpret VVV as QQQ?¶

The following two programs compute a matrix containing vectors that generate the Householder reflectors whose product is Q.

function reflectors_mult(V, x)
    y = copy(x)
    for v in reverse(V)
        n = length(v) - 1
        y[end-n:end] -= 2 * v * (v' * y[end-n:end])
    end
    y
end

function reflectors_to_dense(V)
    m = length(V[1])
    Q = diagm(ones(m))
    for j in 1:m
        Q[:,j] = reflectors_mult(V, Q[:,j])
    end
    Q
end

What happens when R is already upper triangular?

In this case

v=[10]v = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}v=[10​]
(10)

In general, there are two possible reflections. For numerical stability, it is important to choose the one that moves xxx the largest distance, so that it is not too close to xxx itself.

In fact, suppose that in the above figure, the angle of H+H^{+}H+ and the e1e_1e1​ axis is much smaller than xxx or ∣∣x∣∣e1|| x || e_1∣∣x∣∣e1​. Thus, the calculation of vvv represents a subtraction of nearby quantities and will tend to suffer from cancellation errors. We can pick the sign of vvv so that we can avoid such effects by ensuring that ∣∣v∣∣||v||∣∣v∣∣ is never smaller than ∣∣x∣∣||x||∣∣x∣∣.

Householder: An improved algorithm¶

function qr_householder(A)
    m, n = size(A)
    R = copy(A)
    V = [] # list of reflectors
    for j in 1:n
        v = copy(R[j:end, j])
        v[1] += sign(v[1]) * norm(v) # <--- here we pick the sign of v so that moves it the largest distance
        v = normalize(v)
        R[j:end,j:end] -= 2 * v * v' * R[j:end,j:end]
        push!(V, v)
    end
    V, R
end

Summary:¶

  • Classic Gram-Schmidt: Usually very poor orthogonality.

  • Modified Gram-Schmidt: Depends upon condition of AAA. Fails completely when AAA is singular.

  • Householder triangularization: Always good orthogonality and backward stable.
