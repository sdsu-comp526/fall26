2. Orthogonality¶

2.1 Orthogonal matrices¶

If two vectors xxx and yyy are such that xTy=0x^T y = 0xTy=0 then we say xxx and yyy are orthogonal (or “xxx is orthogonal to yyy”).

A vector is said to be normalized if ∥x∥=1\lVert x \rVert = 1∥x∥=1. If xxx is orthogonal to yyy and ∥x∥=∥y∥=1\lVert x \rVert = \lVert y \rVert = 1∥x∥=∥y∥=1 then we say xxx and yyy are orthonormal.

A square matrix with orthonormal columns is said to be an orthogonal matrix (or orthonormal matrix).

We typically use QQQ or UUU and VVV for matrices that are known/constructed to be orthogonal.

Orthogonal matrices are always full rank -- the columns are linearly independent.

The inverse of an orthogonal matrix is its transpose:

QTQ=QQT=I.Q^T Q = Q Q^T = I .QTQ=QQT=I.
(2)

Orthogonal matrices are a powerful building block for robust numerical algorithms.

2.2 Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization¶

For many applications, we find ourselves interested in the column spaces of a matrix AAA:

⟨a1⟩⊆⟨a1,a2⟩⊆⟨a1,a2,a3⟩…\langle a_1 \rangle \subseteq \langle a_1, a_2 \rangle \subseteq \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle \ldots⟨a1​⟩⊆⟨a1​,a2​⟩⊆⟨a1​,a2​,a3​⟩…
(3)

The idea of QR factorization is the construction of a sequence of orthonormal vectors, q1,q2,…q_1, q_2, \ldotsq1​,q2​,… that span these successive spaces.

Thus, suppose we want to find an orthogonal basis for the span of the columns of AAA:

[a1∣a2]=[q1∣q2][r11r120r22]\Bigg[ a_1 \Bigg| a_2 \Bigg] = \Bigg[ q_1 \Bigg| q_2 \Bigg] \begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} \\ 0 & r_{22} \end{bmatrix}[a1​∣∣​a2​]=[q1​∣∣​q2​][r11​0​r12​r22​​]
(4)

Given a1,a2,…a_1, a_2, \dotsa1​,a2​,…, we can construct vectors q1,q2,…q_1, q_2, \ldotsq1​,q2​,… and entries rijr_{ij}rij​, by an iterative process of successive orthogonalization.

A naive algorithm¶

function gram_schmidt_naive(A)
    m, n = size(A)
    Q = zeros(m, n)
    R = zeros(n, n)
    for j in 1:n
        v = A[:,j]
        for k in 1:j-1
            r = Q[:,k]' * v
            v -= Q[:,k] * r
            R[k,j] = r
        end
        R[j,j] = norm(v)
        Q[:,j] = v / R[j,j]
    end
    Q, R
end

A variant with more parallelism¶

We are performing the following (from right to left):

w=(I−q2q2T)(I−q1q1T)v=(I−q1q1T−q2q2T+q2q2Tq1q1T)v=(I−[q1∣q2][q1Tq2T])v\begin{align} w = (I - q_2 q_2^T) (I - q_1 q_1^T) v &= (I - q_1 q_1^T - q_2 q_2^T + q_2 q_2^T q_1 q_1^T) v \\ &= \Bigg( I - \Big[ q_1 \Big| q_2 \Big] \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T \\ q_2^T \end{bmatrix} \Bigg) v \end{align}w=(I−q2​q2T​)(I−q1​q1T​)v​=(I−q1​q1T​−q2​q2T​+q2​q2T​q1​q1T​)v=(I−[q1​∣∣​q2​][q1T​q2T​​])v​​
(5)

Here I−qiqiTI-q_iq_i^TI−qi​qiT​ removes the component of a vector in the direction qiq_iqi​, assuming qiq_iqi​ is a unit vector. From right to left, we are first applying I−q1q1TI-q_1q_1^TI−q1​q1T​, which removes the q1q_1q1​ component. Then we apply I−q2q2TI-q_2q_2^TI−q2​q2T​, which removes the q2q_2q2​ component. In the above, the cross term vanishe as q1⊥q2q_1 \perp q_2q1​⊥q2​.

This operation can be factored in blocks to expoloit a bit of parallelism. Let’s call this classical Gram-Schmidt.

function gram_schmidt_classical(A)
    m, n = size(A)
    Q = zeros(m, n)
    R = zeros(n, n)
    for j in 1:n
        v = A[:,j]
        R[1:j-1,j] = Q[:,1:j-1]' * v
        v -= Q[:,1:j-1] * R[1:j-1,j]
        R[j,j] = norm(v)
        Q[:,j] = v / norm(v)
    end
    Q, R
end

Classical Vs Modified Gram-Schmidt¶

Why does the order of operations matter?¶

We are performing the following (from right to left):

w=(I−q2q2T)(I−q1q1T)v=(I−q1q1T−q2q2T+q2q2Tq1q1T)v=(I−[q1∣q2][q1Tq2T])v\begin{align} w = (I - q_2 q_2^T) (I - q_1 q_1^T) v &= (I - q_1 q_1^T - q_2 q_2^T + q_2 q_2^T q_1 q_1^T) v \\ &= \Bigg( I - \Big[ q_1 \Big| q_2 \Big] \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T \\ q_2^T \end{bmatrix} \Bigg) v \end{align}w=(I−q2​q2T​)(I−q1​q1T​)v​=(I−q1​q1T​−q2​q2T​+q2​q2T​q1​q1T​)v=(I−[q1​∣∣​q2​][q1T​q2T​​])v​​
(6)

which can be factored in blocks and is not exact in finite arithmetic.

Let’s look at this a bit more closely. In the classical Gram-Schmidt (CGS), we take each vector, one at a time, and make it orthogonal to all previous vectors. If an error is made in computing q2q_2q2​ in CGS, so that q1Tq2=δq^T_1q_2=\deltaq1T​q2​=δ is small, but non-zero in finite arithmetic, this will not be corrected for in any of the computations that follow, and it will actually be propagated. In this case, v3v_3v3​ will not be orthogonal to q1q_1q1​ or q2q_2q2​.

Because of rounding errors, in classical Gram-Schmidt, Qk−1Q_{k−1}Qk−1​ does not have truly orthogonal columns. In modified Gram–Schmidt (MGS) we compute the length of the projection of w=vkw = v_kw=vk​ onto q1q_1q1​ and subtract that projection (and the rounding errors) from www. Next, we compute the length of the projection of the computed www onto q2q_2q2​ and subtract that projection (and the rounding errors) from www, and so on, but always orthogonalizing against the computed version of www.

Right-looking modified Gram-Schmidt¶

Each outer step of the modified Gram-Schmidt algorithm can be interpreted as a right-multiplication by a square upper-triangular matrix.

function gram_schmidt_modified(A)
    m, n = size(A)
    Q = copy(A)
    R = zeros(n, n)
    for j in 1:n
        R[j,j] = norm(Q[:,j])
        Q[:,j] /= R[j,j]
        R[j,j+1:end] = Q[:,j]'*Q[:,j+1:end]
        Q[:,j+1:end] -= Q[:,j]*R[j,j+1:end]'
    end
    Q, R
end
